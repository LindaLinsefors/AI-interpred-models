Transformers trained on text from a known Python program: what reverse engineering recovered

From the Goodfire parameter-decomposition team (experiments of October 2026). Written for readers outside the team. It assumes familiarity with transformers and KL divergence, and nothing about our tools. paper.pdf gives the same story with more detail and references.

Short answer.
  • We wrote a ~450-line Python program (grammar plus word lists) that generates simple children's stories, trained small transformers on its text, and then tried to recover the program from the trained network. Because we wrote the generator, we know the right answer.
  • Parts of the network line up with the program's decision points. After the weights are split into rank-1 pieces, single pieces are needed exactly where the generator may copy a character's name or close a quote (F1 = 1.000). Other pieces mark where it may say "she" or "he" for the last-named character (precision 1.00, recall 0.85).
  • LLM agents wrote Python programs that reproduce the network closely, without seeing the generator. Swapped in for the network's parts, the programs reach a KL from the network of 0.044 nats per token (a program built on the decomposition) and 0.011 (a program written without one). For reference, the true generator, lightly smoothed, scores 0.010 and a 4-gram language model 0.40. A low score means the program matches the network's outputs; it does not by itself mean the program is close to the generator.
  • The recovered programs are not clean explanations. They are readable parsers of the story grammar plus large fitted tables; the 0.011 program has more than ten times as many fitted numbers as the network has weights. The agents could also read word classes off the vocabulary, because the tokenizer numbers words class by class. Character-name identity is a dense code that no short rule describes. On a harder generator with hidden variables, the programs recovered the visible world but not the hidden rules.

1. The generator

The generator writes lowercase stories over a 1,358-word vocabulary. Each grammar rule is a Python coroutine that yields the exact probability distribution of the next word. Word classes never overlap, so replaying any text recovers the generator's full state and its exact next-word distribution. Its entropy is 2.26 nats per word. Besides a grammar with subject–verb agreement ("the dog runs", "the dogs run", "i am"), the text has four features that need memory beyond the last few words:

  • Names introduced and copied: a story opens with 1–3 characters ("a girl named nadia and a boy named paul ."), whose names come back later.
  • Pronouns: "he/she/his/her" refer to the gender of the most recently named character, sometimes ~100 words back.
  • Quotes: opened and closed by the same " token.
  • Counted lists: "she made a list of four things : 1 …, 2 …, 3 …, 4 … ." The announced length decides when "," turns into ".".

once there was a girl named nadia and a boy named paul and a girl named ella . both paul and ella play and love nadia . she rests . the ants take ella . she teaches her camel at the tower . … a heavy chair says , " i am gray . " she made a list of four things : 1 a hammer , 2 the cat , 3 a fox , 4 the mice . the mouse reads a baker . … [EOS] once there was a girl named emily . …

The list rule, as the generator writes it 
def make_list(s: State) -> Program:
    """'a list of three things : 1 a red ball , 2 the cats , 3 some cakes .'"""
    yield from say("a list of")
    s.list_length = lx.NUMBER_WORDS.index((yield [(1.0, NUMBER)])) + 2
    yield from say("things :")
    for k in range(1, s.list_length + 1):
        s.list_item = k
        yield from say(str(k))
        yield from noun_rest((yield [(1.0, Words("determiners", ["the", "a", "some"]))]))
        yield from say("," if k < s.list_length else ".")
    s.list_item = s.list_length = 0

The tokenizer is word-level: each word or punctuation mark is one token, so "per word" and "per token" are the same here. It numbers the vocabulary class by class (function words, then girl names, boy names, nouns, plural nouns, places, adjectives, verbs, …), so a word's token id reveals its class.

2. The trained models

We trained a 2-layer and a 4-layer Llama-style transformer (model width 192, MLP width 768, 6 attention heads; the 2-layer model has about 1.1–1.3 million weights) on 500M words of generator text. Both learned the generator almost exactly: KL(generator ‖ model) is 0.0017 nats per word for the 2-layer model and 0.0018 for the 4-layer one. Positions whose next word is a copied name, a pronoun, a list number or list-ending punctuation, or a closing quote make up 15% of the text, but they carry 54% of a trigram model's KL from the network. These positions are where a description that misses long-range state fails.

3. What is inside: pieces that mark the generator's decisions

Parameter decomposition splits each weight matrix into many rank-1 pieces ("components"). Each piece reads one direction of its input and writes one fixed direction. Alongside the pieces, the method learns for every token which pieces are needed to reproduce the network's output there (their "causal importance"), and it is trained so that only a few are. For the 2-layer model, about 54 of its 4,224 pieces are needed per token. Below, a piece is active at a token when it is needed there, and its activation is the number it reads.

We replayed the generator on held-out text and labelled every position with the choice the generator was facing, for example "a character's name may come next". For each label we found the single piece whose being active best predicts the label, and report its precision, recall and F1.

These pieces encode "this choice is open now", not the outcome. The quote-closing piece predicts that the next word is " with precision 0.61, close to the generator's own 0.6 probability of closing a quote there. The she/he pieces are active only when the last-named character has that gender, even when that character was named ~100 words earlier. Persistent states such as "inside a quote" have no single always-on piece.

Side by side: the generator and programs written for single pieces

For each active piece, an LLM agent (Claude Haiku 5.5) that saw the network, its training text and the piece's activations wrote a short Python program predicting the activation token by token. It never saw the generator. Below, each program sits next to the generator code it corresponds to. "Fit" is the share of the variance of the piece's activation that the program explains on held-out text (1 = perfect, 0 = no better than a constant). Layers are numbered from 0, so "layer 1" is the second of the two layers.

The piece's activation is −0.29 at a sentence end inside a quote: exactly where the generator decides whether to close the quote. The program carries one bit, as the generator's in_quote does.

# Fires on the noun naming each numbered list item, e.g. "1 a hen , 2 a short dry witch".
PLURAL_VALUE, SINGULAR_VALUE = 0.76, 0.62

def step(carry, prefix, upstream):
    words = [s.strip() for s in prefix.token_strings]
    value = 0.0
    if is_item_noun(words[-1]):
        for gap in (2, 3, 4):              # the list number sits `gap` tokens back
            if words[-1 - gap].isdigit() and words[-2 - gap] in (",", ":") \

    return carry, {SUBJECT: value}

This piece is active on each list item's noun, and its program finds those nouns. What decides "," against "." is whether the item number equals the announced length. In an audit of ten per-piece programs, none tracked both numbers. Five of the ten agents noticed that their piece changed with list position. None kept it, and at least four said it barely improved their fit. Most of the list-ending error came from two such programs, for pieces 658 and 442 (fit 0.98 each; both are in code/). The positions where the count decides the next word (a list number, or the "," or "." after an item) are 6% of the text, so the fit barely sees them. With all programs swapped in, those positions cost 0.88 nats each. Adding one feature, "this is the last item", to the two programs cut that to 0.12, while their fit changed by 0.001.

Which name to copy is the hardest part. At name positions, the layer-1 attention heads write each of the 195 names as its own dense vector. The per-name vectors have no clusters: 59 directions hold 99% of their variance, and no direction holds more than 4%. About 106 pieces carry parts of this name code; at the layer-1 attention output, about 65 of them each respond to roughly 30 names. The agents' instructions discouraged lookup tables. In 12 of 13 audited sessions the agent fitted a table over names, then dropped it, and the median of a related fit score on these pieces was 0.07. When tables over words were allowed, it rose to 0.85.

4. One program for the whole model

To test whether programs together capture the network, we swap them in for all the parts they cover at once (here, all 816 active pieces of the attention and MLP output matrices), run the hybrid network, and measure

KL(network ‖ hybrid) = mean over positions of  Σw pnetwork(w) · log [ pnetwork(w) / phybrid(w) ]

in nats per token. Two reference points use the same formula with the hybrid replaced by another model: a Kneser–Ney 4-gram model trained on the same text scores 0.40, and the true generator scores 0.010. The generator assigns probability zero to most words at most positions, so for this reference it is mixed with a uniform distribution at weight 10−6. (The 0.0017 in section 2 is the KL in the other direction, which needs no smoothing.) These are reference points, not a floor: a program fitted to the network can score as well as the generator without being any closer to it.

  • One program per piece (816 programs) scores 0.317, already better than every n-gram model. Most of its error is at name and list-ending positions, for the reasons in section 3.
  • Merged programs. A team of two stronger agents (an OpenAI GPT-6 model and Claude Opus 5.5) merged the per-piece programs into one, so that shared state such as a list counter is computed once. With tables banned, the merged program got list endings exactly right (KL 0.000) and ordinary tokens nearly so (0.002), but failed at name positions, for a joint 0.837. In a second attempt with tables over words allowed, the team kept the earlier per-piece list programs instead of its own list counter, and reached 0.044. That program is ~8.1k lines; 591 of its 816 outputs reproduce earlier per-piece programs and 225 come from refitted tables of story characters and roles. Its remaining error splits evenly between list endings and name positions.
  • Without a decomposition, the same kind of agent team wrote one program for the four layer outputs directly, in about an hour (effort was not equalised with the decomposition route). It reached 0.011. It is a ~230-line streaming parser of the story grammar (word class, roles, agreement, quotes, list counter, cast of characters) plus ~16M fitted coefficients (82 MB) that map its features linearly onto the layer outputs. Its word lists match the generator's word for word and in the same order, consistent with reading them off the class-ordered vocabulary.

5. A harder generator with hidden variables

A second generator (~750 lines) adds a visible world (characters move between places, take and give things, count coins, talk, and answer questions about the story) and four hidden families that the text never states: a theme per story (16 options), a temperament per character (4), a hidden kind per noun (12) that restricts which adjectives and verbs fit, and 8 "laws" (XOR rules over hidden halves of the place and name sets). Ignoring them costs 0.154 nats per word. Of the model sizes we trained, a 6-layer, width-384 transformer learned this world best (KL 0.012 from the generator); smaller ones learned the laws less well.

What the readable part recovered: the visible world (cast, genders, places, who holds what, coins, lists, questions and answers, who "he"/"she" refer to), and the existence of 16 story themes, which it infers from word-to-theme affinities. What it did not recover as rules: temperament (approximated by each character's event history), noun kinds (a per-noun lookup instead), and the laws (no explicit representation). The decomposition route was not matched in effort: it had one inexpensive agent per piece and no merging round (one was started and stopped early).

6. Limits

  • Fitting outputs is not explaining the computation. Every program here reads only the text, never the network's internal values. So when one of the network's pieces is edited (held at its mean, or given its value from another text), the hybrid gets that edited piece, but the downstream programs cannot respond. For the 12 most influential pieces, the hybrid's KL from the edited network was 0.36 (mean) and 0.50 (swap), about the same as simply ignoring the edit (0.41 and 0.60). This is expected by construction. It shows that a good KL on ordinary text cannot tell a program that tracks the network's computation from one that only predicts its text.
  • The programs are partly tables. In every accurate program, the readable part is a parser of the grammar, and the map from parsed facts to the network's numbers is fitted. On the harder generator, the hidden rules end up inside fitted numbers.
  • The vocabulary gave away the word classes. Because token ids are grouped by class, a program can recover the generator's word lists without inferring them. How much of the grammar the agents found independently is therefore unclear; the parsing logic and the name positions are their own.
  • Scope. Small models, two synthetic languages, one or a few attempts per setting; the decomposition and no-decomposition routes had unequal effort.
