How a 432-parameter RNN finds the second-largest number

An explainer from Goodfire's parameter-decomposition team for readers outside the team. It assumes you know what an RNN and a ReLU are. The full write-up is paper.pdf in this folder; every program mentioned is in code/.

Short answer. The core algorithm is clear. The network approximately keeps the largest value so far and the runner-up, plus a rotating clock whose angle tracks how many steps ago the largest value arrived. Four comparator units test each new input against thresholds that swing with that clock. At the end, the comparators move votes from the largest input's position to the runner-up's.

A complete short explanation of what the network computes, including its mistakes, is a largely negative result so far. A short idealised account built on these parts solves the task but reproduces only 5% of the network's mistakes. A full account, the network's own recurrence rewritten in the same named quantities, reproduces 96% of them, but it is not shorter than the network, and most of its coefficients have no interpretation.

Details

The task, the network, and how we test an account definitions

Task. The input is ten numbers, each drawn independently from a standard normal distribution, read one per step. The correct output is the position (0–9) of the second-largest.

Network. A one-layer ReLU RNN without biases from ARC's AlgZoo, a set of tiny trained networks released without explanations as test cases for interpretability. ARC calls this one M16,10 and described units 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7.

h_t = ReLU(w·x_t + W·h_(t−1)),   h_(−1) = 0,   logits = U·h_9

w has 16 weights, W has 256 and the readout U has 160: 432 in all. Only the last state h_9 reaches the readout.

Tests. An account is a program that outputs a 16-number hidden state at every step.

  • Agreement: the account's state replaces the network's at every step, the network's own readout U turns the final state into an answer, and we count how often it equals the network's answer.
  • Mistakes reproduced: of the inputs the network gets wrong, the fraction where the account gives the same wrong answer.
  • Single-unit replacement: run the real network, but overwrite one unit at every step with the account's value for it. If the account describes that unit correctly, the network's answers should survive.

Agreement alone is a weak test: the plain rule "answer the true second-largest" already agrees 95.3% of the time while reproducing none of the network's mistakes.

The algorithm, step by step memory, comparisons, the idealised account

Unit groups. Comparators: units 1, 6, 7, 8. Pulses: units 4, 5, 9. Wave units: the other nine (0, 2, 3, 10–15).

Memory means the quantities the state carries from step to step. They are not stored in dedicated units: they are a fixed linear re-coding of the 13 non-pulse units, comparators included. Memory holds approximately a level M for the largest value so far and a level S for the runner-up; a clock that rotates about 42° per step and restarts on a new maximum, so its angle tracks the maximum's age (steps since it arrived); and further oscillating "history waves" that carry information about earlier candidates.

Comparisons.

  • Unit 4 fires when the input beats the maximum, roughly 10·max(x − M, 0). Its real threshold is close to, but not exactly, M.
  • Comparators output g·max(θ − x, 0) with gains g ≈ 10.5, 11, 13, 12; each threshold θ is set by memory, lies near S and M, and swings with the clock.
  • Unit 5 fires on the step after a record or a new runner-up. Unit 9 fires on the step after a record.

The idealised account (code/idealised_account.py) writes this down literally:

M' = max(M, x)          S' = max(S, min(M, x))          (both start at 0)
clocks z_M, z_S: multiply by e^(0.74i) (42.4°) each step; reset to 1 when the value is replaced;
                 on a record the old maximum's clock passes to the runner-up
comparator j:  g_j · max(θ_j − x, 0),  θ_j = S + (M − S)·(A_j + B_j·Re z_M + C_j·Im z_M + D_j·[record last step])
unit 4: 10·max(x − M, 0)    unit 5: 12·max(x_(t−1) − S, 0)    unit 9: 1.5·M·[record last step]
wave units: ReLU of a half-integer mix of 12 features (levels, z_M, and z_S with its 2nd and 3rd powers)

Its threshold and mixing coefficients were fitted by gradient descent on training inputs, minimising the task cross-entropy through the network's readout plus 0.02 × the squared difference from the network's hidden states, then rounded. Because the levels start at 0, it cannot locate a negative runner-up (about 1% of inputs have at most one positive value).

M, S and age are computed from the inputs; unit values are the real network's. Unit 4 is only roughly 10·max(x − M, 0): at step 6 that formula gives 4.0, and the network gives 5.7.

How the vote works here. The other 12 units give the largest input's position +47, and the comparators give it −35. The runner-up's position wins because of the comparators (+39); the other units give it −2. Neither group picks the answer alone: the comparators' largest vote is at position 2 (+57), which the other units cancel (−46). Position 9, the newest input, gets the largest votes of both groups (+51 and −82); the network's exact rewrite reads position 9 through a separate route, from the current input, the runner-up level and the record pulse.

Across inputs. On 16,076 random inputs whose maximum and runner-up both arrive before position 9, looking at positions 0–8:

  • the other units' largest vote is at the maximum's position on 87% of inputs (median +44 there);
  • the comparators' most negative vote is at the maximum's position on 76% (median -28);
  • at the runner-up's position the medians are +15 from the other units and +12 from the comparators;
  • the comparators favour the runner-up over the maximum on 97% of inputs, the other units on 8%.

So the other units vote mainly for the maximum, the comparators move votes from the maximum to the runner-up, and the answer comes from the sum.

The idealised account solves the task but agrees with the network less often than the plain rule, and when any one of its comparators replaces the real one the network keeps only 33–49% of its answers. The full account passes every test.

Size. Counting every numeric literal, readout excluded: idealised 171, full 334 (285 of them coefficients). The network has 272 weights before the readout. So the full account is not a compression; it changes coordinates so that the parts can be named and removed one at a time.

How the full account relates to the idealised one. The full account writes the recurrence of the 13 non-pulse units in the eigenbasis of their recurrent matrix. Each eigenvalue is a mode: a real one is a component scaled by that factor each step; a complex pair rotates by an angle and shrinks to a radius each step.

The levels, main clock and gains have close counterparts. But 256 of the full account's 285 coefficients are dense couplings (how units and pulses read the modes, and how pulses write into them), and we have no interpretation for most of them.

What the idealised account leaves out removal experiments and the network's mistakes
  • Exact angles matter little, but the shared frequency does. Tying the two extra rotations to multiples of their own base, 41.6°, costs nothing. Putting all angles on the idealised account's single 42.40° frequency keeps 98.3% of answers but only 84% of mistakes.
  • Idealising the levels or the clock's decay is costly without refitting (89–92% of answers, 59–64% of mistakes). With the levels held exact and everything else refitted, the original authors recovered 98.5% and 86%, so that detuning can be compensated elsewhere.
  • The extra modes matter: without the short-lived clock 51% of answers survive; without the alternating mode or the transient, 89–91%.
  • The calibration matters: comparators that read only the levels and main clock keep 36%; no clipping write-back keeps 60%.
  • Controls: removing unit 5 or unit 9 is costly, but the idealised account has both pulses too.

These experiments show what the full account needs to reproduce the network. They do not show which part causes which mistake; we have not tested that.

The network's mistakes are near-ties. 77% of its mistakes fall on the 19% of inputs where two of the three largest values are within 0.05 of each other, and 63% name the third-largest. The idealised account behaves differently (original notes, 19,776 inputs): it is better than the network when the second and third values are within 0.05 (90.8% against 77.8%), worse when both gaps are at least 0.05 (97.3% against 98.4%), and 383 of its 690 mistakes pick a value ranked 4th–10th, against 161 of the network's 931.

The network remembers more than the top three. (1.2, 0.1, 0, 1.23, 1.26, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0) and the same with the second number changed to 1.1 have the same three largest values at the same positions. The network answers 3 (correct) for the first and 0 for the second; the idealised account answers 3 for both. In the full account most of the difference comes from the short-lived clock.

Two efforts with overlapping results

Two efforts in our team, both carried out by AI agents, reached overlapping descriptions. One decomposed the weight matrices into simple rank-one pieces, each needed on few inputs, and interpreted them. The other asked agents, without the decomposition, for a program reproducing the hidden state; their first program was the exact rewrite, and the idealised and full accounts came later. That later write-up adopted the decomposition paper's role names. The two agree on what each unit does but group and name some differently:

Limits and open questions
  • No short faithful account. The idealised account is short but not faithful; the full account is faithful but not short. The original authors' searches for a short recursion that passes the single-unit and mistakes tests failed, though a failed search is not a proof.
  • No mechanistic accuracy estimate. ARC's standard is a deductive estimate of the network's accuracy whose error matches random sampling at the same compute. Neither account gives one.
  • Fitted thresholds do not replace units. Threshold formulas fitted to the real comparators reach R² > 0.99, yet replacing any one of them keeps only 78–88% of the network's answers.
  • Error causation is untested.
  • Some unit roles are vague. Units 3, 10, 11 and 15 are described only as parts of the wave group.
